# Hollowbrook: story, colour and things to do

> Research for the developer and both lanes (see `AGENTS.md`). It was written from five web research briefs and a fact check of their main claims. The suggestions are proposals: the user decides what gets built. Asset needs that follow from it are queued in `docs/ASSET-REQUESTS.md`.

*Farm Village analysis, 2026-10-08. It draws on five research briefs, a fact check, and a read-only look at the repo (branch `codex/v0.4-orchard`, commit f0d8886, plus Codex's draft notes in `docs/RESEARCH-DIRECTION.md`, not yet committed when this was written). No code. Evidence labels: **strong** = peer-reviewed or large studies; **expert** = designers' reports; **company data** = a studio's own numbers; **weak** = small studies or popular claims.*

## 1. In short

1. People enjoy farm games alone when they feel free, capable and needed, and when nothing bad can happen. Hollowbrook is already safe. It is weak on "needed and noticed".
2. Story keeps players when it gives the chores a reason: one open question, small answers often, and payoffs you can see. Hollowbrook's mystery contradicts itself and never pays off.
3. Story spine: **"Where the Brook Begins"** (*uống nước nhớ nguồn*). It is one question that lasts all eight stages and follows the map. Lantern Night (Trung Thu) and Tết become yearly rituals.
4. "Fancy" does not mean more gold. It means clear light and dark steps, strong colour where it matters, warm light with cool shadows, a little shine, and gold saved for earned moments. Today the daytime farm sits at one lightness, so it looks flat.
5. Colour psychology is weak science. Use colour as story feedback (ruins faded, restored places warm), not as a mood switch.
6. A world feels big when every object reacts to a touch and every item has several uses. In Hollowbrook, fish lead nowhere, picking fruit is silent, and much of the village is only scenery.
7. JOURNEY.md has the right ladder. What is missing is links between systems and payoffs, not more systems.
8. Do three things first: fix the story's facts and order; do a colour, light and gold pass; make the village notice what the player did.

## 2. Why people love these games when they play alone

- **Free, capable, connected (strong).** Feeling free and capable goes with enjoyment. Feeling connected predicts enjoyment and coming back, but in that study it meant other human players (Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006). Characters who respond to you meet part of that need, probably not all of it (Tyack & Wyeth 2017).
- **Pressure matters more than hours (strong).** Large studies with real play data found that hours played had little or no effect on well-being. Feeling pushed to play went with feeling worse.
- **Safety, plenty, softness (expert).** This is how Project Horseshoe defines coziness. It warns against rewards that work like bribes, needy things you must look after, notifications, harsh effects and showy luxury.
- **Company without other players.** Animal Crossing's creator made it after he felt lonely in a new city, so that a family could share one village at different times. Hollowbrook has no other players, so the villagers must do this. Add one light way to share with real people: a postcard.

| Need | The player feels | Now | Missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Safety | "Nothing bad happens if I leave." | Nothing is lost while away; no energy, ads or paid currency | Ada says it out loud; a warm welcome back; no lines that make the player feel guilty |
| Freedom | "I choose today." | Many systems; the Next chip is only a suggestion | Toys with no goal; calm things to do at night; a clear place to stop |
| Getting good | "I used that cleverly." | Mill and bakery chains; the truck and fruit stand pay more | Real choices for each good (fish and the stall have none); levels 8–60 unlock nothing; stage progress that counts what you already did (people speed up near a goal: strong) |
| Needed and noticed | "They saw what I did." | Orders in each person's voice; 45 heart scenes; letters | Nobody uses your gifts or mentions what you built; 10 remarks written for Mai and Gus never show; hearts change nothing |
| Plenty | "There is enough to share." | Generous pace; tips | Gifts on doorsteps, a shared table, every window lit |
| Ritual | "My little routine." | Today board, streak garden, day and night on the real clock | A warm end to each session; festivals every year |
| Curiosity | "What happened at the sluice?" | Ellis's letters; clues in heart scenes | Clues in the right order; payoffs you can touch |
| Making it mine | "It looks like mine." | Name, figure, shirt; decorations; photo mode; albums | Your name in lines; keepsakes on the porch; before-and-after pages (people value what they built, if it works out: strong) |

## 3. Story

### What works and what fails in other games

- **A reason to start, and a world that changes.** Stardew added its restoration story to give players "a stronger sense of purpose", with no time limit. Each finished room repairs a bridge or the bus. Every top-100 grossing US casual game released since 2020 has a renovation layer (GameRefinery).
- **Being needed.** Cozy Grove's writer calls it "a fantasy of service": as its ghosts heal, colour returns to the island.
- **Private lives through farm work.** Stardew's Shane recovers through his chickens.
- **Story as a wrapper.** Playrix says almost all active Gardenscapes players follow its story. That is company data, and partly built in, because players must spend stars on story tasks.
- **Sadness in small doses.** Spiritfarer's director says it is "only 10–15% dramatic".
- **What fails:** stories that run too long (fewer than 20% of players finished My Time at Sandrock), mysteries that never resolve, people who only give orders, and early clock gates (one of the complaints about Animal Crossing: New Horizons).

### Hollowbrook now: an honest diagnosis

**What is strong:** a warm premise, a big cast with clear voices, tested Vietnamese pronouns, short cards, 21 letters, 45 heart scenes, Gus's arc, and the rule that nobody is mean.

**Problems** (small slips are listed in section 6):
1. **The facts disagree.** One letter says "a new padlock", another "old brass with an E … from my own shed". Gus says "thirty years ago", but Sam's scene says "twenty years late". The letters already solve the sluice (the key is under the school bell), while STORY.md plans a different answer for chapters 6–8 (a flour company, and buying back the water rights).
2. **Clues come out of order.** Letters fire on unrelated counters. Ellis's padlock detail (sent after 3 fish) can come before he even mentions the sluice. Gus's confession (after 6 goals) can come before chapter 3.
3. **No payoffs.** The festival is a toast. Ellis never appears. Nobody finds the key or opens the sluice. Pia's rusty key and Zara's flour sacks lead nowhere.
4. **The world does not change with the story,** apart from lit windows and repairs.
5. **The pictures are taken from far away.** Ada with the key is a speck. The stage site is a dim field, because there is no stage model.
6. **The story stops at chapter 5.** After that the game says "Every project of this version is done."

### Recommended direction: "Where the Brook Begins"

*Uống nước nhớ nguồn* means "when you drink water, remember its source." The core feeling is gratitude to your roots: the grandparents who stayed, the families who left, and the brook itself. One question carries the whole game: **why does the brook run low, and where does it begin?** Each part of the answer is a place on the map, so every unlock is also a story step. The billion at the end is the value of a living valley. This builds on JOURNEY.md, where Pip already finds the spring in Act IV. It does not replace it.

| Stage | Small question, answered in its chapter | What the player sees |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Homecoming | Why did everyone leave? The mill closed. | First loaf, hens named, a lit window |
| 2 Orchard | Why does Ada plant trees she will never eat from? (*ăn quả nhớ kẻ trồng cây*) | Blossom; each tree has a tag with who planted it |
| 3 Meadow | Who owns the land upriver? A city flour company. | The dry meadow turns green once watered |
| 4 River | When was the sluice shut? The summer the mill closed. | The dock; river fish come back |
| 5–6 Village, company | Can we buy the water back? What happened on Lantern Night? | The sluice opens, the wheel turns, Lantern Night returns, Ellis comes home |
| Act III | Should the meadow become a factory? | Ada's golden-bird bedtime tale; the co-op |
| 7 Pine Ridge | Why is the spring weak? The slopes were cut. | Replanted terraces; Nana Tuyết |
| 8 Valley | Where does the brook begin? | Pip finds the spring; lanterns over both villages; four generations at the Tết table |

**Alternatives.** *The Lantern Promise* (the village learns to gather again) is warm, but its mystery ends at chapter 9 and leaves Acts III–V with nothing to drive them. *The Golden Bird* (*Ăn khế trả vàng*) teaches modest wealth, but a bird that pays gold pulls the game toward greed. Tell it only once, as Ada's bedtime story before Mr Albright's offer.

### Concrete changes

1. **One truth about the past.** For example: *thirty years ago, in a dry summer, the mill owner paid young Gus to lock the sluice with a padlock from Ellis's shed. The mill closed anyway. That autumn a storm knocked over the festival lanterns, and Gus got the children out. Later the city flour company bought the upriver land and added its own new lock.* Now both padlock lines are true, because there are two locks. The key under the school bell opens the old one. The new one needs the water rights (chapter 8). Say "thirty years" everywhere.
2. **Chain the clues.** Each mystery letter waits for the letter before it and for its own chapter.
3. **Let the player do the payoffs.** Tap the school bell and the key drops out. Open the old lock and find the new one behind it. On Lantern Night, Ellis walks in at the gate (he needs a model and a portrait for this).
4. **Split the festivals.** Now: a school celebration with a short visit from Ellis (Codex's draft `docs/RESEARCH-DIRECTION.md` suggests this too). In chapter 9: the real Lantern Night on a rebuilt stage.
5. **Story never blocks play.**
   - The world changes first, then a three-line card explains it.
   - No card opens during a sweep or in build mode.
   - Letters and heart scenes wait in the mailbox.
   - At most one small story beat a day on the Today board, kept until the player reads it.
   - Chapters open through deeds, never through the clock.
   - Each act ends with a "the story rests here" card.
   - Story pictures show their subject close up.
6. **Human touches.** Use the player's name in Ada's letters. Give each chapter a person with a need and a visible "after". For example, Marisol wants a place to sit outside the clinic, and afterwards Hazel and Ada really sit there.
7. **Mr Albright (open question 1 in JOURNEY.md).** Keep the choice, make both paths respectable, and make nothing permanent. You can buy the meadow back later, and Mai is sad for a while, not gone.

### Vietnamese culture, used warmly

- **Lantern Night is Hollowbrook's Tết Trung Thu,** Vietnam's children's festival. It has star lanterns (*đèn ông sao*) in many colours, carp lanterns, mooncakes, a lion dance that comes in when the family invites it, and Cuội on the moon. The children lead the parade. Ada bakes mooncakes from your eggs and wheat, with lotus seeds from Mai's farm. Gus lights the first lantern. After that it returns every real Mid-Autumn (next around 15 September 2027).
- **Tết is a homecoming,** which is already the game's frame. Aim an update at late January 2027. Tết is around 6 February; check a Vietnamese lunar calendar. Activities:
  - sweep the house before New Year's Eve;
  - wrap *bánh chưng* by Ada's fire;
  - lay a five-fruit tray from your orchard;
  - choose peach blossom or yellow *mai*;
  - give the children red envelopes;
  - pick your first visitor of the new year (Mai, Gus or Cora) for a New Year scene.
- **Kitchen Gods' day,** when the Kitchen Gods ride a carp to heaven: Pip and Ada release your golden carp into the brook, with a short recap, "Our year in Hollowbrook".
- **Rice by the brook** (stage 4) feeds *bánh chưng* and rice-dough figures (*tò he*). **Later**, your own parent comes home from the city for the last Tết: four generations at one table (*tứ đại đồng đường*).
- **Rules:** the family altar and red envelopes never pay coins. Festival items return every year. No chapter waits for a real date.

## 4. Colour, light and the "fancy" feel

### What makes games look rich

- **Light and dark steps first.** Valve's Team Fortress 2 paper: "muted colors dominating and small areas of saturation", and "shadows go to cool, not black". Royal Match's pieces are "distinct in both color and shape".
- **Strong colour where it matters** (roofs, fruit, people, flowers) on a calmer ground. Calm means fewer bright areas competing, not grey: a muted palette looks pale.
- **Warm light and cool shadows,** as at golden hour (James Gurney).
- **Shine and life.** Mario Kart 8's developers point to moving kart parts, dirt on the tyres and skid marks. Mario Kart World has 24 racers so the courses do not feel "lonely".
- **Rare gold, and the best colour saved for later.** Hearthstone's golden cards change nothing in play, but they shine and cost more. Journey saved bright blue skies for its ending.

### What the research supports

- **Yes (strong, lab studies):** lightness and saturation (how strong a colour is) affect feelings more than hue does (Valdez & Mehrabian 1994; Wilms & Oberfeld 2018). People like the colours of things they like, such as clear sky and clean water, and dislike olive and brown (Palmer & Schloss 2010). Effects at medium-to-high strength that are tied to success beat no effects and extreme effects. Making effects bigger without tying them to success lowered motivation (Kao 2020; Kao et al. 2024).
- **No:** "gold makes players happy or spend more" and "yellow means joy" are not supported. The main review calls colour psychology "nascent" (Elliot & Maier 2014). How many people link yellow with joy ranges from 88% in Finland to 6% in Egypt.
- **Culture:** in Vietnam, red and gold mean luck. White is for funerals (headbands, robes), but white flowers are fine. Write *xanh lá* (green) or *xanh dương* (blue), never just *xanh*.

### What the code shows today

- By day, grass, paths, road, plaza, UI gold, coins and ripe wheat all sit at lightness 72–87 (L*, where 0 is black and 100 is white). Gold on grass has a contrast of only 1.03:1. Only soil (36) and water (54) are dark. So things stand out by hue alone, which fails in sun glare and for colour-blind players.
- Both daytime lights are warm and the bounce light is olive, so shade looks warm grey. 3D materials are matte, and gold appears only on knobs and finials. The night look is good: keep it.
- The richer palette in Codex's draft `docs/RESEARCH-DIRECTION.md` is a good start, but its path and grass have the same lightness (L* 64 and 63), so they would look flat too.

### Rules for gold

1. **Gold means "you earned this":** coins, reward panels, the level badge, chapter seals, lanterns, the golden carp, a gilded sign on a reopened building. The orange panel headers can stay, but reward gold must look richer than them.
2. **Gold is always shaded, outlined and moving:** five steps, a dark edge, a glint every 2–4 seconds, and coins that spin once when they land.
3. **Budget** (starting values): under about 5% of the screen in normal play; about 20% for a few seconds in big moments; full-screen rays only at the eight stage endings.
4. **Put gold on contrast** (dusk violet, night blue, dark wood). Below gold, use a ladder: wood, silver-blue, gold, radiant gold. Rare finds can use other colours, such as a sky-blue egg.
5. **Golden light counts as gold:** give the truck's send-off three seconds of golden-hour light, and make real dusk (18:30) a small daily ritual.
6. **Red with gold only for luck and festivals** (lantern red #E2412F, lantern gold #FFD36A). Show wealth as plenty, not as piles of gold. The final family photo gets the game's only fully gold frame.

### Celebration ladder

Rules for all tiers:
- Every effect is tied to a success.
- Events that happen at the same time merge into one.
- No more than three flashes a second.
- Reduced motion swaps bursts for fades.

| Tier | Examples | How often | Length | Look and sound | Gold |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small | Harvest, eggs, fruit picked (silent today), a fish | Dozens per session | Under 1 s; never blocks play | Pop, a few bits, an icon flies to the barn; notes rise along a harvest streak | Only if coins are earned |
| Medium | Order filled, truck back, heart level, golden carp (today it gets the same pop as a perch) | 3–10 per session | Up to 2 s; tap to skip | Coin pour, counter roll, a glow on the building, a villager waves, Pip's face reacts | Brief |
| Big | Level up, a family moves in, a building restored, a chapter done | 1–2 per session; queue the rest | 3–5 s; skip after 1 s | Colour wave, 3 s of golden-hour light, confetti in Hollowbrook colours, the family reacts, a short fanfare | Up to about 20% of the screen |

Stage endings get a longer scene you can skip, and a family photo. Stop the confetti on ordinary repairs. The shape of the ladder comes from the feedback studies above; the exact numbers are my starting values.

## 5. Making the world feel big: interactions

### The patterns

- **Many uses per item.** Stardew's egg sells, goes into eight recipes and a mayonnaise machine, fills a bundle, works as dye and answers quests.
- **Simple rules, multiplied.** Breath of the Wild's chemistry is three rules applied to most objects. Stardew's honey takes the flavour of the nearest flower.
- **Toys before goals.** Animal Crossing's tree shake gives different drops, has daily limits and sometimes drops comic wasps. Jesse Schell asks: "If my game had no goal, would it be fun at all?"
- **The world answers back.** Animal Crossing villagers comment on your things. In Untitled Goose Game, villagers put back what you moved.
- **Explain combinations; hide only bonuses.** This audience enjoys completing and decorating most and experimenting least (Quantic Foundry), so characters should explain the combinations.
- **On a phone, the object picks the action.** Stardew's phone version picks your tool for you, and Hay Day "came together" once chores became touch gestures.

**One set of touches:**
- tap = the useful action;
- hold = play or care (shake, pet, hug);
- drag = sweep or rub;
- flick = throw;
- give = drag a good onto a person.

Play never spends goods. Rewards from play are capped per object per day, and a small sparkle marks today's surprise.

## 7. Traps to avoid

- **Gold everywhere,** flat yellow, or wealth shown as piles of gold. **Too much juice:** confetti on every repair, celebrations stacked on top of each other, scenes you cannot skip.
- **Punishing absence:** wilting, spoiling, hungry pets, mice or crows that take real goods, or villagers asking "where have you been?" (in one small interview study, this made players feel bad). If something must wear out, make the worn state pretty, as Hay Day did with its withered bee bushes.
- **Fixed play times and fear of missing out:** rewards tied to set times, festival items you can only get on one real date, streaks that reset.
- **Story that blocks play,** and mysteries that contradict themselves or never pay off.
- **Chores that grow faster than the tools for them:** a tap per bed, hens every 45 seconds, an order board that nags. Playrix removed rent collection, repairs and re-plowing from Township.
- **Hidden rules you need to progress,** too many menus, more than three goals at once.
- **Status shown by colour alone,** red flashing, or red used as an error colour.
- **A pale wash,** or everything at full strength and one lightness. **Bloom on phones** (the budget is 120 draws and 300,000 triangles).
- **Culture as costume or currency:** red envelopes that pay the player, the family altar used as a shop, white headbands at a party, plain *xanh* in text.
- **Copying other games:** take My Dear Farm's pattern (a few happy or sleepy faces, optional, never sad), not its art or its pastel palette.
- **Notifications as demands** ("your farm misses you").
